Lab 9: Choropleth versus Cartogram

Global economic size, mapped two ways

A coordinated comparison of 2025 nominal GDP across the 50 largest economies. Color preserves geographic context; area makes economic magnitude harder to overlook.

1 · Dataset description

A deliberately bounded comparison

The analysis uses the supplied top-50 table of 2025 nominal GDP at current prices, measured in billions of U.S. dollars. The CSV is joined to world country boundaries through ISO-3 identifiers. The 50 records are included as statistical observations; every other mapped feature is retained as geographic context and labeled No data.

GDP is strongly right-skewed, so both maps use a logarithmic color scale. The cartogram uses the same GDP field as its area weight. Selecting a country in either view highlights the matching ISO-3 feature in both views.

4 · Design and comparison

The encoding changes the question the map makes salient

In the choropleth, the United States and China are the most visually prominent because their large GDP values receive the darkest colors, while the underlying map preserves each country’s location, shape, and relative geographic context. Germany, India, Japan, and the United Kingdom remain easy to locate even when their areas are geographically small. The logarithmic scale prevents the many lower-ranked economies from collapsing into one indistinguishable shade.

The cartogram makes the same ranking more forceful through area. The United States and China expand substantially, whereas many smaller economies contract, so economic magnitude becomes easier to compare but geographic recognition becomes harder. The cartogram distorts shape, distance, and land area; it therefore supports questions about relative economic size and rank rather than questions about spatial adjacency or physical extent. The choropleth is better for locating economic concentrations and regional patterns. The cartogram is better for judging how much global output is concentrated in a few economies. Moving from color to area changes perception because area occupies physical space and turns numerical inequality into a change in the map’s geometry.
